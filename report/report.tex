	\section{Problem Description}\label{sec:problem}

	\paragraph{The decision problem, in our own words.}
	A hospital plans a timetable over a horizon of $D$ days ($D\in\{14,21,28\}$ in the public instances). Each day has three shifts:
	early, late, and night. The input describes patients waiting for surgery, occupants already in the
	hospital (with fixed rooms and stays), rooms, nurses, surgeons, and operating theatres (OTs). For
	each admitted patient, the hospital chooses an admission day, a room that is kept for the whole
	stay, and an OT for the surgery. The surgery takes place on the admission day. For every room that holds
	a patient or an occupant in a given shift, the hospital assigns exactly one on-duty nurse. The competition rules of \citet{ihtc2024spec} define the IHTP. It joins three classic subproblems \citep{ceschia2011,cardoen2010,burke2004} that share the same beds, staff, and theatres. These are patient admission scheduling (PAS), surgical case planning (SCP), and nurse-to-room assignment (NRA).
	Some patients are mandatory and must be admitted within a time window. Others are optional and may
	be postponed. A plan is feasible when it meets the eight hard constraints \Hc1 to \Hc8. Among feasible plans, the goal is to minimize a weighted sum of the eight soft costs \Sc1 to \Sc8. Section~\ref{sec:approach} lists both.
	
	\paragraph{Sets, parameters, and decision variables.}
	The tables below list the notation. The decision variables are binary, the standard way to write an assignment model \citep{wolsey1998}. We keep the room and the admission day in one variable ($x$) because a room is
	held for the whole stay. The theatre is kept in a separate variable ($y$) because it is used
	only on the admission day.

	\section{Solution Approach}\label{sec:approach}

	\paragraph{Decomposition: how and why we split the problem.}
	A single MIP over all four variable families, including the nurse variables, is too large to solve within our budgets. Even the admission-only subset exhausts its work limit on the largest instances. We count the variables of Section~\ref{sec:problem} from the instance sizes. The binaries alone come to about $4\times10^4$ on a median instance and $4\times10^5$ on the largest. We therefore split the problem into an upper layer and a lower layer. This is
	the standard decomposition for the IHTP, and \citet{ihtc2024paper} report it among the strong
	entries to the competition. These entries differ in which part leads the search. The method of \citet{garciatercero2026} is led by local-search neighborhoods. Ours is led by the exact admission MIP, because the admission terms dominate the objective and an exact model packs patients in well (Section~\ref{sec:approach}). The upper layer sets the admission day, room, and theatre
	(variables $x$, $y$, $z$). This fixes all hard constraints except nurse coverage, and it fixes the
	patient-side soft costs \Sc1, \Sc5, \Sc6, \Sc7, \Sc8. The lower layer assigns nurses (variable
	$v$), which fixes the coverage constraint \Hc8 and the nurse soft costs \Sc2, \Sc3, \Sc4. We solve
	the two layers with the following pipeline. The pipeline pairs a heuristic search with exact
	mixed-integer programs, which is the matheuristic approach surveyed by \citet{maniezzo2010}. Each stage is present because the ablation in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} shows that adding it lowers the overall cost. Anything that did not help was dropped. No component is included just to look advanced. Each is kept only if it brings a measured improvement, and otherwise it stays out (one candidate that
	failed this test was not adopted, Appendix~\ref{app:pricing}).
	\[
	\text{construct} \rightarrow \underbrace{\text{PAS-MIP}}_{\text{admission}}
	\rightarrow \underbrace{\text{ALNS}}_{\text{refine}}
	\rightarrow \underbrace{\text{descent}}_{\text{true cost}}
	\rightarrow \underbrace{\text{CP-SAT LNS}}_{\text{capacity}}
	\rightarrow \underbrace{\text{OT-MIP}}_{\Sc5,\Sc6}
	\rightarrow \underbrace{\text{NRA-MIP}}_{\Sc2,\Sc3,\Sc4}.
	\]
	
	\emph{Construction.} We quickly build a first plan. Patients are inserted in order: mandatory before optional, then tighter admission window, longer surgery, and longer stay first. The most constrained patients are placed while resources are least committed. Each takes its cheapest feasible day, room, and theatre. An optional patient is admitted only if its cost
	is below the penalty $W_8$ for leaving it out. A greedy order can fill a surgeon's schedule and leave a mandatory patient unplaced. We therefore repair \Hc5 with ejection chains, the compound-move idea of \citet{glover1996}. We move the blocking patients to free capacity, backtracking at most three ejections deep. We also try up to three cheap randomized restarts from slightly varied insertion orders (early stop once one is feasible; best plan kept). On the one instance in the ablation where construction ends infeasible (i16), the PAS-MIP restores feasibility through its mandatory-equality constraints (the scored +PAS cell in Table~\ref{tab:abl}). The ALNS opening feasibility phase remains a backup. It is a ruin-and-recreate repair in the sense of \citet{schrimpf2000}, emptying and rebuilding the congested region around an unplaced mandatory patient. The construction only has to give a usable starting plan: the construct column of Table~\ref{tab:abl} shows how far it sits from best-known before the optimizing stages run.
	
	\emph{PAS-MIP (the admission core).} This is the decisive stage, because the objective is dominated
	by unscheduled optional patients (\Sc8). Admitting more of them is a packing problem that local search cannot solve well, but an MIP can\footnote{One admission can require many patients to move together \citep{ceschia2011}. A MIP weighs those combinations jointly, where local search stalls \citep{maniezzo2010}.}. Our admission model is the assignment form of the patient admission scheduling problem studied by \citet{ceschia2011}, matching the code that solves it.\footnote{Our code has one difference. It computes unscheduled optionals directly as $1-\sum_{r,d}x_{p,d,r}$ rather than with the explicit variable $z_p$. The two are equivalent. On instances with more than 300 patients the code also clips each optional patient's admission-day window to the eight days after release (mandatory windows are untouched). This pruning keeps the model small at little cost, since the delay penalty \Sc7 already favors early admission.} The model uses the admission variable $x_{p,d,r}$ (created only for
	feasible triples) and a per-room-day gender indicator $g_{r,d}$:
	{\small
		\begin{align*}
			\min\;\; & W_8\!\!\sum_{p\in\mathcal P^{O}} z_p
			+ W_7\!\!\sum_{p,r,d}(d-\rho_p)\,x_{p,d,r} \\[-2pt]
			\text{s.t.}\;\;
			& \textstyle\sum_{r,d}x_{p,d,r}=1\;(p\in\mathcal M),\quad \sum_{r,d}x_{p,d,r}+z_p=1\;(p\in\mathcal P^{O}) && \text{(admit; \Hc5, \Hc6)}\\
			& \textstyle\sum_{p\in\mathcal P^{A}}\sum_{d'\le d<d'+\ell_p} x_{p,d',r}+\mathrm{occ}^{A}_{r,d}\le \mathrm{cap}_r(1{-}g_{r,d}) && \text{(\Hc1, \Hc7, gender A)}\\
			& \textstyle\sum_{p\in\mathcal P^{B}}\sum_{d'\le d<d'+\ell_p} x_{p,d',r}+\mathrm{occ}^{B}_{r,d}\le \mathrm{cap}_r\,g_{r,d} && \text{(\Hc1, \Hc7, gender B)}\\
			& \textstyle\sum_{p:\,u_p=u}\delta_p\!\sum_r x_{p,d,r}\le T^{\mathrm{surg}}_{u,d},\quad
			\sum_{p}\delta_p\!\sum_r x_{p,d,r}\le \textstyle\sum_t A_{t,d} && \text{(\Hc3; \Hc4 in aggregate)}
	\end{align*}}%
	The two gender constraints per room-day force every room-day to hold at most $\mathrm{cap}_r$
	patients of one gender. Rooms and gender are handled exactly here. Theatre packing (\Sc5, \Sc6) is left to the OT-MIP, and the small-weight age mix (\Sc1) to the room choice in the surrogate and to the descent. This keeps the model small. A greedy pass then completes the plan before the feasibility check that decides adoption: cheapest feasible theatre per surgery, nurses filled greedily. The model bounds \Hc4 only in aggregate, so a patient's slot can fail a per-theatre check. That rare patient is re-placed in the cheapest feasible slot. In the
	ablation this stage contributes the single largest drop (Table~\ref{tab:abl}, +PAS column).
	
	\emph{ALNS (Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search).} Adaptive large neighborhood search \citep{ropke2006} repeatedly removes part of the plan\anote{The random, worst-cost, and related removals draw their size uniformly between one and one seventh of the admitted patients, at most 25. That size is large enough for joint moves and cheap to repair. The day and theatre removals clear their whole target. Partial clearing would leave the packing in place.} and
	rebuilds it better; its purpose here is to find improvements that no single-patient move can reach, when several patients must shift together. Our five removal operators each target one weakness. \emph{Random} removal diversifies broadly and re-offers a couple of postponed optionals, which repair may admit. \emph{Worst-cost} removal pulls out the most delayed patients (\Sc7). The \emph{empty-a-day} move clears one admission day so its capacity can be re-packed. The \emph{empty-a-theatre} move empties one theatre on one day, trying to close it (\Sc5). The \emph{related} removal of \citet{shaw1998} extracts the patients most related to a randomly chosen seed (same surgeon, overlapping stay, shared room) so they can be re-packed jointly. Our three repair operators re-insert what was removed, mandatory patients always first. \emph{Greedy} insertion (cheapest feasible slot) is the fast baseline. \emph{Regret-2} insertion places first the patient that would lose the most by waiting (the widest cost gap between its best and second-best feasible placement). \emph{Randomized greedy} samples among each patient's few near-best slots, so the same removal is not always rebuilt the same way. Operators are drawn with probabilities adapted to their recent success, the adaptive scheme that gives the method its name. Each draw earns a graded reward, largest for a new best plan. Every 100 iterations the weights are smoothed toward each operator's average reward. Moves are accepted by the simulated-annealing rule of \citet{kirkpatrick1983}: a worse plan is accepted with a probability that shrinks as the search cools. This lets the search escape local optima early and settle later. The starting temperature comes from thirty probe moves on the instance itself, set so we accept about forty percent of worsening moves at the start; it then decays geometrically for
	the rest of the run. Feasibility is maintained by construction, so the only violation the search can create is an unadmitted mandatory patient, which we handle with a large penalty. At periodic checkpoints (every 1{,}500
	iterations) the search also runs a deterministic admission sweep: every postponed optional whose best slot costs less than $W_8$ is
	inserted. For each one with no feasible slot, a bounded ejection chain is tried. It is kept only if the surrogate cost drops. It gives the search a cheap version of the packing the exact models do. The ALNS has two measured roles. It reduces the secondary costs the admission model does not optimize. Its sweep recovers admissions where the time-limited PAS-MIP left optionals unadmitted. In the +ALNS column of Table~\ref{tab:abl}, the large i27 drop is consistent with recovered admissions (i27 prices each admission at $W_8=500$, Table~\ref{tab:audit}). The column is neutral where its targets are already minimal.
	
	\emph{Descent (true-cost local search).} The ALNS loop scores the fast surrogate, so a short
	descent follows whose accept test is the full objective. For each admitted patient it tries every feasible day, including its current one, each with the
	surrogate-cheapest room and theatre for that day; only the exact current placement is skipped. Only one candidate per day is tried, since each candidate requires a complete
	rescore, with the nurses re-assigned, and the room and theatre choices interact only through the day. The descent takes the
	first relocation that improves and keeps a move only when the complete, nurse-staffed score
	strictly drops. Its purpose is to reduce the nurse terms the surrogate does not price, plus the age mix (\Sc1) left over; its contribution is the +descent column of Table~\ref{tab:abl}.
	
	\emph{CP-SAT LNS (the capacity-creating step).} The single PAS-MIP times out on the largest
	instances (the drop from the +descent to the +LNS column of Table~\ref{tab:abl} shows what the timeout leaves unrecovered). No small move can re-pack a full schedule to fit one more optional patient. We
	therefore free every still-unscheduled optional patient, plus everyone admitted inside a random
	window of days, and re-pack that window with an exact model. The freed set is capped near 110 patients, and the window width scales with instance size. When the set still overflows, admitted optionals are randomly subsampled, never the unscheduled or mandatory patients. This is a large neighborhood search, the
	destroy-and-rebuild scheme surveyed by \citet{pisinger2010}, with an exact re-pack in place of a
	heuristic one. The frozen patients' resource use is subtracted as
	residual capacity, so the small model stays valid. We solve it with CP-SAT, the constraint solver
	in OR-Tools. Its reified constraints (constraints switched on and off by a boolean variable) model the
	gender, open-theatre, and surgeon-transfer indicators directly, and its deterministic time budget keeps the many small repeated solves reproducible. Admissions raise nurse cost, so each round bundles several re-packs, screened on the costs the re-pack model itself counts (\Sc5--\Sc8). The nurse re-solve then runs once per round rather than once per re-pack.
	A round is kept only after the nurses are re-optimized and only if the full objective strictly
	improves, so the stage is monotone. We run four rounds, since profiling found little gain past four (the budget note in \texttt{ihtp/experiments.py}). The cap keeps each re-pack model near one size, so the fixed per-solve budget fits every round. In our experiments this stage lowers the cost in every row of Table~\ref{tab:abl} (+LNS column); it is where the last hard-to-place optional patients are admitted.
	
	\emph{OT-MIP.} Operating-theatre scheduling is its own research field, reviewed by \citet{cardoen2010}. With days and rooms fixed, each day is a small independent assignment problem. We pick the theatre $y_{p,d,t}$ for each surgery to minimize open theatres (\Sc5) and surgeon transfers (\Sc6) under \Hc4. Each day has a few surgeries, so each of these models is tiny and runs under a fixed deterministic per-day work budget. The stage's small remaining gain is the +OT column of Table~\ref{tab:abl}.
	
	\emph{NRA-MIP.} Nurse-to-room assignment is the staffing side of the plan, closest to the nurse rostering problems surveyed by \citet{burke2004}. The roster here is fixed input, and the task is to assign on-duty nurses to rooms. With the layout fixed, the nurse variable $v_{n,r,s}$ places one on-duty nurse per
	occupied room-shift (\Hc8). Overflow variables $o_{n,s}\ge 0$ model excess workload (\Sc4), and the
	link $c_{e,n}\ge v_{n,r_e,s}$ counts distinct nurses per patient, so $\Sc3=\sum_{e,n}c_{e,n}$. The
	objective $W_2\Sc2+W_3\Sc3+W_4\Sc4$ is minimized over the whole horizon, since continuity (\Sc3) couples every shift of a patient's stay; no shift-by-shift solve could trade this off. The greedy nurse assignment is a feasible warm start: a chronological sweep, so a patient's earlier nurse can be picked again for continuity. It
	staffs the rooms with the highest required skill first, while the skilled nurses are still
	lightly loaded, and picks each nurse by marginal cost under the true weights. The sweep, however,
	fixes each shift in turn and cannot look ahead, so this exact solve recovers the skill,
	continuity, and workload cost the sweep misses (Table~\ref{tab:abl}, +NRA column). Both models appear in full in Appendix~\ref{app:models}.

	\paragraph{How the components interact.}
	Three mechanisms tie the stages into one method. First, \emph{warm starts}: every exact model begins from the current plan. The construction seeds the PAS-MIP, the incumbent theatre placement seeds the OT-MIP, and the greedy nurse assignment seeds the NRA-MIP. The current placements enter the CP-SAT re-pack as hints. Each Gurobi solve therefore starts from a known solution it can only improve in its own objective. The round check described next covers the re-pack. Second, a \emph{keep-best incumbent}: from the descent onward, a stage's output replaces the incumbent only when the full evaluated objective strictly improves. The capacity-creating LNS and the final exact solves can never make the reported solution worse. The two stages before them, the PAS-MIP and the ALNS, hand their result forward directly (PAS accepted when feasible, ALNS feasible by construction). They re-decide the dominant admission terms rather than refine a fixed plan. Third, a \emph{two-speed objective}: the heuristic's inner loops and the admission models score a fast upper-layer surrogate that excludes the nurse terms. The incumbent is updated at the ALNS checkpoints, each LNS round, the descent, and the final exact solves. Each update re-scores the full objective with the nurses re-solved, greedily at the checkpoints and in the descent, and exactly thereafter. The surrogate makes many iterations possible, and the full-objective checks keep the reported cost
	accurate. Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipeline} summarizes one full run.

	\begin{algorithm}[t]
		\caption{One run of the pipeline: $S$ is built once (line 1), then improved stage by stage;
		each \textsc{small-caps} call is a stage of Section~\ref{sec:approach}. $z(S)$ is the full
		objective, all eight soft costs with the nurses re-assigned before scoring (greedily, except
		at the \textsc{Nra-Mip} calls, which are exact). Lines 2--5 re-decide the admissions and
		hand their result forward; from line 6 on a change is kept only if it strictly lowers $z$,
		so the plan never gets worse. Every stopping rule is an iteration, round, or work budget,
		never wall-clock, so a fixed seed reproduces the run (same platform and solver versions;
		Section~\ref{sec:results}).}\label{alg:pipeline}
		\begin{algorithmic}[1]
			\Require an instance and a seed
			\Ensure a feasible plan; every plan we report is additionally re-checked by the competition's official validator
			\State $S \gets \textsc{Construct}$ \Comment{a fast greedy plan; hardest-to-place patients inserted first}
			\State $P \gets \textsc{Pas-Mip}(\text{warm start }S)$ \Comment{exact model re-decides every admission, day, and room}
			\State fill $P$'s theatres and nurses greedily \Comment{the admission model leaves both to this quick pass}
			\State \textbf{if} $P$ is feasible \textbf{then} $S \gets P$ \Comment{adopted outright: admissions carry most of the objective}
			\State $S \gets \textsc{Alns}(S)$ \Comment{reshuffles days, rooms, and theatres, with periodic admission sweeps; worse plans accepted early on}
			\State $S' \gets \textsc{Descent}(S)$; \textbf{if} $z(S')<z(S)$ \textbf{then} $S \gets S'$ \Comment{one-patient moves, judged on the full score}
			\For{$4$ rounds} \Comment{the capacity-creating step; each round bundles several re-packs, each on a fresh random window}
				\State $S' \gets \textsc{RepackSlice}(S)$ \Comment{free the not-yet-admitted optionals $+$ a day window, capped near 110 freed; CP-SAT re-solves the slice, the rest stays frozen}
				\State $S' \gets \textsc{Nra-Mip}(S')$; \textbf{if} $z(S')<z(S)$ \textbf{then} $S \gets S'$ \Comment{exact nurse assignment; keep the round only if cheaper once staffed}
			\EndFor
			\State $S' \gets \textsc{Nra-Mip}(\textsc{Ot-Mip}(S))$; \textbf{if} $z(S')<z(S)$ \textbf{then} $S \gets S'$ \Comment{final clean-up: exact theatre assignment, then exact nurse assignment}
			\State \Return $S$
		\end{algorithmic}
	\end{algorithm}

	\paragraph{Hard constraints and feasibility.}
	Feasibility rests on two things: the construction and the exact models keep every hard constraint,
	and the official validator confirms it. \Hc2 (compatible rooms) and \Hc6 (admission window) are held by
	construction, because we only ever create a variable $x_{p,d,r}$ for a legal day and a compatible
	room. \Hc1 (no gender mix) and \Hc7 (room capacity) are enforced exactly by the two gender
	constraints of the PAS-MIP, and are re-checked on every heuristic move. \Hc3 (surgeon time) is enforced by the PAS-MIP and re-checked on every heuristic placement.
	\Hc4 (theatre time) is enforced by the PAS-MIP only in aggregate (total surgery duration per day
	within total theatre capacity). The exact per-theatre bound is enforced when theatres are assigned, and checked on every placement and move. The OT-MIP re-optimizes it within capacity, and the official validator confirms it on every final solution. \Hc5 (all
	mandatory admitted) is secured by the construction, the ejection repair, and the equality
	constraint in the PAS-MIP. \Hc8 (nurse presence: an on-duty nurse covers every occupied room-shift)
	is met by the greedy nurse assignment and the NRA-MIP, which chooses $v_{n,r,s}$ only among nurses on
	duty in that shift. The official validator, our ground truth for feasibility, implements \Hc8 as two
	checks: \texttt{UncoveredRoom} (every occupied room-shift has a nurse) and \texttt{NursePresence}
	(the assigned nurse is on duty in the shift). We never claim a solution feasible on our own evaluator alone. Every reported solution is re-run through the official validator. We report it as feasible only when that run shows zero violations.
	
	\paragraph{Objective and soft constraints.}
	We re-implemented the official objective in Python and checked it against the validator with a golden test. Our evaluator and the official validator score the same published solutions, and every component must match exactly (Section~\ref{sec:impl}). The search therefore optimizes the
	objective the competition measures. Each soft cost is reduced
	at the stage that controls it. Admission (PAS-MIP and CP-SAT LNS) reduces unscheduled patients
	(\Sc8) and admission delay (\Sc7). The OT-MIP reduces open theatres (\Sc5) and surgeon transfers
	(\Sc6). The NRA-MIP reduces under-skill (\Sc2), continuity of care (\Sc3), and excess workload
	(\Sc4). The descent reduces the age mix (\Sc1, the spread between the oldest and youngest age
	groups sharing a room on a day). The weight $W_8$ on unscheduled patients is by far
	the largest (150 to 500 across instances, against at most 50 for the others), so the objective is driven mostly by how many patients are admitted.
	
	\section{Implementation}\label{sec:impl}

	\paragraph{Architecture.}
	The solver is a Python package (\texttt{ihtp/}). Its modules are instance parsing, a solution model with incremental cost updates, a full objective evaluator, the constructive and ALNS heuristics, the four exact models, and the experiment scripts. The incremental model keeps running
	totals of cost and capacity, so testing one move costs time proportional to what the move touches,
	not to the whole instance. One command (\texttt{./run.sh}) builds the validator, runs the tests,
	solves all instances, computes the certified lower bounds, and writes every result. Every number
	and table in this report can therefore be rebuilt by running the code on a fresh copy of the
	repository, with the recorded solver versions and licenses. The appendix experiments have their
	own scripts under \texttt{extensions/}.
	
	\paragraph{Solvers, libraries, and tools.}
	We use \textbf{Gurobi 13.0.2} for the admission, theatre, and nurse MIPs and for the lower bound. Its deterministic work-based stopping (\texttt{WorkLimit}) also underpins our reproducibility.
	Gurobi is the only commercial solver we use, under an academic license, as permitted
	by the assignment. We use the open-source \textbf{OR-Tools CP-SAT 9.15} for the capacity-creating
	search, and \textbf{NumPy 2.5} for the data arrays. Feasibility is checked by the official
	\textbf{IHTP\_Validator} \citep{ihtc2024site} (the C\texttt{++} program from the competition), which we compile from its
	source.
	
	\paragraph{Instance and solution formats.}
	The program reads the official IHTC-2024 instance JSON files \citep{ihtc2024site} and writes the solution JSON in the
	required schema (the admission day, room, and theatre per patient, and the nurse per occupied
	room-shift). The required results CSV (one line per instance, \texttt{i01,<objective>}, or \texttt{i01,infeasible}) is written by the same scripts.
	
	\paragraph{Validation.}
	Our Python evaluator guides the search. It matches the official validator component by component (our golden test) on the published i01 reference solution (zero violations, all eight soft costs, and total 3842). It also matches on seven further reference solutions spanning the size range, where we check every hard and soft component. Since i01's reference solution has zero surgeon transfers and zero excess workload, these seven test the components and edge cases (near-horizon truncation, $\sim$500-patient instances) that i01 cannot. These are feasible published solutions, so this is an agreement test on valid states, not a proof of equivalence for every solution the search visits. It need not be: every solution we report is re-scored by the official validator binary, and we keep the validator's own objective value as the reported number.
	
	\paragraph{Computational environment.}
	Every run records the machine on its own, in the file \texttt{environment.txt}. Each run's results store the solver versions the objectives depend on, and the hardware the runtimes depend on. The submitted run
	used an Apple M5 processor (10 cores), 34.4 GB of memory, and macOS 26.5.2 (arm64), with Python
	3.12.13, Gurobi 13.0.2, OR-Tools 9.15.6755, and NumPy 2.5.1. The full run (all 30 instances, five seeds each, across the ten cores) took about 5.8 hours (20{,}953 seconds) of wall-clock, for the solve and validation. The certified bounds were computed in a separate step, not included in this figure. The per-instance runtime in Table~\ref{tab:res} is the solver time summed over the five seeds. The 150 single-threaded runs share the ten cores, so their solver times sum to about 58 hours, roughly ten times the wall-clock. Each solver gets one thread. Several solver threads working together can return a different solution under a fixed budget, which would break fixed-seed reproducibility. We fill the cores by running instances in parallel instead.

	\paragraph{Setup, feasibility, and objectives.}

	(6 July 2026) stored with the code, so our gaps stay fixed even if the competition site changes
	later. The metaheuristic is randomized, so we run five fixed seeds per instance and report the best of the five as the headline value, with the seed spread in Appendix~\ref{app:dist}. The four added seeds recover about one point of mean gap.

	\paragraph{Reproducibility.} The method is deterministic.\footnote{This does not conflict with the randomized metaheuristic: the search makes random choices, but each is drawn from a pseudorandom generator that the run's seed fixes, so the same seed reproduces the same run.} It uses no wall-clock stopping rule. 	\section{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{sec:discussion}

	\paragraph{Where the gap comes from and where the computing effort goes.}
	We compared our solutions with the best-known solutions component by component, summed over all 30
	instances. The largest cost in the objective is
	unscheduled patients (\Sc8), but that is not where we lose. A direct count of admitted optionals
	(not the weighted \Sc8 cost, whose weight varies by instance) shows we admit 2\,995 against the
	reference solutions' 2\,933, and our weighted \Sc8 is about 2\% lower. The gap is therefore not
	about which optionals we admit. Admission delay (\Sc7) costs us 17\% of the gap. Most of the
	rest comes from the nurse side: continuity (\Sc3) 39\%, under-skill (\Sc2) 30\%, and workload
	(\Sc4) 22\%, about 90\% of the net gap (78\% of the gross positive differences). Appendix~\ref{app:comp}
	and Table~\ref{tab:adm} give the breakdown and the per-instance counts. We diagnosed
	two causes, each with a controlled experiment. The nurse-budget sweep ran during development;
	the notes in \texttt{ihtp/experiments.py} summarize it, and its per-instance records were not
	archived. The registered probe of Appendix~\ref{app:structure} later re-measured the effect
	with stored records.
	First, the nurse MIP was given too little solver time. A re-solve with a larger work budget on
	the same fixed layout cut nurse cost by up to 16\% on the hard instances, and by 0\% on the small
	ones that were already optimal. This led to the size-scaled nurse budget the pipeline now uses, 600 to 800 work units rising with rooms times days. The aggregate gap moved from 9.2\% at the old flat budget of 120, to 7.9\% at a flat 600, to 7.8\% with the scaling. It applies to the final nurse solve; the LNS-round re-solves keep a smaller cap, since they only decide round acceptance. Second, a cause remains after the budget fix, and we first read it as purely structural. The admission stage does not see the nurse cost its choices create. A later probe splits it (Appendix~\ref{app:structure}). A larger nurse budget alone closes 6\% to 63\% of the remaining gap on the largest-gap nurse-heavy instances, and none on a small control. Part of what remains is therefore solver budget, and the rest is structure. These two also carry the computing effort. The admission MIP stops fitting inside its work budget as instances grow, which is why the CP-SAT re-pack stage exists. The sweep showed the nurse MIP starved, which is why that budget is the one we scale with instance size.
	More seeds help only slightly (Appendix~\ref{app:dist}); the budget notes in
	\texttt{ihtp/experiments.py} record the same for longer budgets, which the probe above
	corrected for the nurse budget on the largest instances.

	\paragraph{The coupling test: a nurse-aware admission audit.}
	The simplest explanation would be that the nurse-blind admission stage
	admits patients whose staffing cost exceeds the penalty they avoid. A standalone audit tests that reading directly, leaving the pipeline untouched (Appendix~\ref{app:audit}). The pipeline can admit an optional patient, but no stage can later un-admit one against the full objective. The audit adds that missing move. It postpones each admitted optional, one at a time, from our final solutions, and keeps a removal only if the complete objective, nurses included, strictly improves. On i01 and the five largest-gap instances, over 1\,061 admitted optionals, it accepts zero removals. The smallest margin is 58 cost units (i27). Every audited admission is individually justified. The damage is not in how many patients we admit. It is in where we put them, meaning which room and which day, and that drives continuity (\Sc3), the largest nurse term. Two fixes follow: pricing an estimate of the placement-side nurse cost into the admission
	objective, and looping the admission and nurse models rather than running them once.
	\citet{garciatercero2026}, competition finalists, keep the models separate in the same way, with IP-based operators inside a local search \citep{ihtc2024paper}.
	Appendix~\ref{app:pricing} reports a pre-registered test of a static form of the first step,
	priced into the heuristic upper layer. It passes its declared criteria, but a placebo control\anote{A placebo control removes the claimed active ingredient, here the real nurse information, and keeps everything else the same; if it does as well, the ingredient did not help \citep{shadish2002}. A perturbation is any change to the numbers guiding the search.} attributes its small aggregate gain to the perturbation itself rather than to the nurse information, which points away from static prices. Appendix~\ref{app:structure} then splits the remaining nurse gap between solver budget and the coupling itself.
	
	\paragraph{Ablation.}\anote{An ablation is a controlled experiment that measures what one part of a method contributes \citep{fawcett2016}. We add the stages one at a time, in the fixed order the pipeline runs. The seed and the per-stage work budgets stay fixed, and we record the objective after each. The PAS-MIP and the CP-SAT LNS share the admission terms. Every other stage targets its own cost terms, so one stage's drop cannot come from giving another more time. The single seed shows one deterministic path; Appendix~\ref{app:dist} reports the seed spread.} Table~\ref{tab:abl} adds the stages one at a time; each stage's measured role is stated with that stage in Section~\ref{sec:approach}. The same standard held beyond the stages: the nurse budget rose only
	after the controlled experiment above showed it binding, and Appendix~\ref{app:pricing}'s priced
	layer was not adopted after failing its placebo control. The four instances span the size range (i04 to i27), and the stages enter in pipeline order, so neither the subset nor the order is tuned to the outcome.

	\paragraph{Certified optimality gap.} The best-known values are upper bounds, not proven optima, so
	a gap to them cannot say how close we are to the true optimum. We therefore compute a valid lower
	bound of our own\anote{A certified optimality gap is the distance between our objective and a proven lower bound on the true optimum, as a percentage of that bound \citep{wolsey1998}. The true optimum cannot sit below the bound, so the real gap to it is at most this figure (Appendix~\ref{app:bounds}).}. We solve a relaxation that keeps the admission decision and the two binding
	resources (surgeon-day and theatre-day time) with the costs they drive, and drops the rest.
	No dropped constraint or non-negative cost can raise the optimum, so the relaxation's proven dual bound is a valid lower bound \citep{wolsey1998}, the argument \citet{ceschia2011}
	use for patient admission scheduling (Appendix~\ref{app:bounds}). This gives a certified gap:
	the true optimum lies between the bound and our objective. The bound is
	loose because it ignores the nurse and age-mix costs. Both our certified gap (57\%) and
	best-known's own (46\%) are therefore large and track each other across instances. We read
	the looseness as mostly in the bound, since the pooled best of 32 teams is unlikely to sit 46\% above the true optimum.
	
	\paragraph{Strengths and limitations.} Every public and hidden run produced a feasible solution, with gaps a few percent above the pooled best of 32 teams. The results rebuild from the repository. Our development and tuning used the 30 public instances, so the headline results are measured on instances that shaped the method. A later pre-registered run on the competition's hidden instances (Appendix~\ref{app:hidden}) adds a held-out measurement: feasible on all 150 runs, at a 6.10\% aggregate gap against a smaller benchmark pool. Claims beyond these two instance sets remain untested. The certified lower bound is valid but loose, so we locate each true optimum only within the reported interval. The numbers are tied to the recorded solver versions, budgets, and hardware, and to a Gurobi license, which the exact models and the certified bound require. A different solver version or CPU architecture may change them. We report seed spreads (Appendix~\ref{app:dist}), not formal confidence intervals. The open modeling problem is separate from these limits on the evidence. Appendix~\ref{app:pricing}'s static-pricing test left the admission-nurse coupling unresolved. The coupling loop and a larger nurse solve are the future work we propose.
	
	\paragraph{Conclusion.}
	A two-layer matheuristic solves the IHTP feasibly on all 30 public instances and comes within a
	fraction of a percent of best-known on the closest ones. The aggregate gap is 7.8\%, and 6.10\%
	in one held-out run on the hidden instances (Appendix~\ref{app:hidden}). We built the method from our own reading of the problem rather than adapting a published solution, and we kept each part only when a measurement supported it. The dominant cost sat in admissions, a capacity-packing problem, so we solved it with an exact model. Our component analysis placed the remaining gap in the nurse layer. We traced it to a solver budget later found still binding on the largest instances (Appendix~\ref{app:structure}), and to a model coupling. On the audited instances no single admission is worth undoing, and a fixed price on where patients go gains no more than its placebo. The coupling loop is the next step.

	\section{The operating-theatre and nurse models}\label{app:models}
	The report gives the admission model (PAS-MIP) and describes the last two exact models in
	words. The formulations below are our own and match our implementation in Python. They follow the standard assignment-model forms for operating-theatre scheduling \citep{cardoen2010} and for the nurse-to-room subproblem as the competition defines it \citep{ihtc2024paper}. We adapted both to this competition's costs and constraints.
	
	\paragraph{OT-MIP, per day.} For a fixed day $d$, the surgeries are the patients admitted on day
	$d$, and $t$ ranges over the theatres available on day $d$ ($A_{t,d}>0$). Variables: $y_{p,t}$ (surgery $p$ in theatre
	$t$), $\omega_t$ (theatre $t$ used), $h_{u,t}$ (surgeon $u$ uses theatre $t$), and $\tau_u\ge 0$
	(the number of extra theatres of surgeon $u$).
	{\small
		\begin{align*}
			\min\;\; & W_5\textstyle\sum_t \omega_t + W_6\sum_u \tau_u\\
			\text{s.t.}\;\;
			& \textstyle\sum_t y_{p,t}=1 \quad(\text{each surgery }p) && \text{(one theatre)}\\
			& \textstyle\sum_p \delta_p\,y_{p,t}\le A_{t,d} && \text{(\Hc4)}\\
			& \omega_t\ge y_{p,t},\qquad h_{u_p,t}\ge y_{p,t} && \text{(open / surgeon flags)}\\
			& \tau_u\ge \textstyle\sum_t h_{u,t}-1 && \text{(\Sc6 transfers)}\\
				& y_{p,t},\,\omega_t,\,h_{u,t}\in\{0,1\},\ \tau_u\ge 0 && \text{(domains)}
	\end{align*}}%
	
	\paragraph{NRA-MIP.} With the layout fixed, $(r,s)$ ranges over occupied room-shifts. Variables:
	$v_{n,r,s}$ (nurse $n$, on duty in shift $s$, covers room $r$), $o_{n,s}\ge 0$ (workload of $n$ in
	shift $s$ above the cap), and $c_{e,n}$ (entity $e$, a patient or occupant, is seen by nurse $n$).
	The skill shortfall $\mathrm{gap}_{r,s,n}=\sum_{e\in(r,s)}\max(0,\mathrm{req}_{e,s}-\sigma_n)$ is a constant. The workload demanded in room $r$ at shift $s$ is $w_{r,s}=\sum_{e\in(r,s)}w_{e,s}$. The input gives occupants the same per-shift workload and skill-requirement data as patients.
	The entities $e\in\mathcal P\cup\mathcal O$ are the admitted patients and occupants. Each entity $e$ occupies room $r_e$, and $e\in(r,s)$ means $e$ is present in room $r$ during shift $s$. Requirements are indexed from each entity's start. For a patient admitted on day $d_0$, stay-shift $j$ is global shift $s=3d_0+j$. Occupants are indexed from day~0.
	{\small
		\begin{align*}
			\min\;\; & W_2\!\!\sum_{r,s,n}\!\mathrm{gap}_{r,s,n}\,v_{n,r,s}
			+ W_3\!\sum_{e,n} c_{e,n} + W_4\!\sum_{n,s} o_{n,s}\\
			\text{s.t.}\;\;
			& \textstyle\sum_{n\in\mathcal N_s} v_{n,r,s}=1 \quad(\text{each occupied }(r,s)) && \text{(\Hc8)}\\
			& o_{n,s}\ge \textstyle\sum_r w_{r,s}\,v_{n,r,s} - L_{n,s} && \text{(\Sc4)}\\
			& c_{e,n}\ge v_{n,r_e,s}\quad(\text{each shift }s\text{ of }e\text{'s stay}) && \text{(\Sc3)}\\
				& v_{n,r,s},\,c_{e,n}\in\{0,1\},\ o_{n,s}\ge 0 && \text{(domains)}
	\end{align*}}%
	The greedy nurse assignment warm-starts the NRA-MIP, and the current theatre placement warm-starts the OT-MIP. Each solve therefore begins from a feasible incumbent, which gives branch and bound an upper bound to prune against from the first node \citep{wolsey1998}.
	
	In both models every auxiliary counter ($\omega_t$, $h_{u,t}$, $\tau_u$, $o_{n,s}$, $c_{e,n}$) is only bounded from below. Minimization then drives each to its exact count at optimality. This is the standard linearization device \citep{wolsey1998}.

	\section{Certified lower bounds}\label{app:bounds}
	We bound the true optimum from below by relaxing the exact model. A dropped constraint can only lower the optimum, so a relaxation's optimum is a valid lower bound \citep{wolsey1998}. \citet{ceschia2011} bound patient admission scheduling the same way. Our relaxation is the admission model of Section~\ref{sec:approach} with the room dropped (binary $x_{p,d}$, admit $p$ on day $d$). The large-instance clipping of optional windows noted in Section~\ref{sec:approach} is not applied here. A valid relaxation needs every legal admission day, and \texttt{ihtp/bounds.py} keeps them all. Over the whole horizon this model is paired with the per-day theatre variables of Appendix~\ref{app:models}. Those variables are the binary theatre choice plus the open-theatre and surgeon-transfer indicators, all under the per-theatre \Hc4. The relaxation keeps the admission rules \Hc5 and \Hc6 and the surgeon-day \Hc3 and theatre-day \Hc4 time limits. It also keeps the four costs those resources drive, \Sc5, \Sc6, \Sc7 and \Sc8. Those four are all it minimizes. It drops the room and gender assignment (\Hc1, \Hc2, \Hc7), the bed capacity they imply, and the nurse coverage (\Hc8). The same drops remove the non-negative costs \Sc1 (age mix) and \Sc2 to \Sc4 (nurse). The removals enlarge the feasible region, and every cost they drop is non-negative. Neither change can raise the optimum, so the bound is valid.

	Each relaxation runs on Gurobi with a deterministic $\mathrm{WorkLimit}=120$, $\mathtt{Threads}=1$, $\mathtt{Seed}=0$ and Gurobi's default MIP gap, with no wall-clock limit. The reported bound is $\lfloor\mathrm{ObjBound}\rfloor$, the proven dual bound. Branch and bound keeps a proven floor under every subproblem it has not yet closed, so this bound stays valid when the work limit stops the solve early. On i01 to i06 the relaxation solves to proven optimality, so the bound is its exact optimum. On the other 24 (i07 to i30) the bound is the dual bound at the work limit. That bound is still valid, but looser. Per-instance status is in \texttt{results/bounds.csv}. The aggregate certified gap is pooled over instances, $g=\big(\sum_i \mathrm{ours}_i-\sum_i \mathrm{LB}_i\big)/\sum_i \mathrm{LB}_i$. The table gives each instance's objective, best-known, bound and both certified gaps. Best-known's own gap shows how much of ours comes from bound looseness (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

	\section{The nurse-aware admission audit}\label{app:audit}
	The audit is a standalone script (\texttt{extensions/postpone\_audit.py} in our repository); it
	consumes the final validated solutions from \texttt{results/} and modifies neither the pipeline
	nor its results. The pipeline works one way only. Every admit-or-postpone decision compares a patient's own cost against the penalty $W_8$, and it never looks at the nurse terms. Once a patient is admitted, no stage re-examines that decision
	individually against the full objective (the CP-SAT LNS accepts whole rounds, not single
	admissions, and the descent relocates admitted patients but never removes one). The audit adds
	the missing move. It works through the admitted optional patients in a seeded random order, so the audit is reproducible. For each such patient $p$, it removes $p$ and re-scores the complete objective. Changing one thing at a time is how an ablation assigns credit \citep{fawcett2016}. Before scoring, the nurse assignment is re-solved with the same greedy nurse fill that the pipeline's true-cost descent uses. The removal is kept only if the total strictly
	drops; otherwise $p$ is restored exactly. This is first-improvement descent over a one-move
	neighborhood, with the randomized order and the repeat-until-no-change rule that stochastic
	local search uses \citep{hoos2004}. Passes repeat until one finds no improvement, with a cap of four as a safeguard against a long sweep; in every run reported here the first pass already found none, so the cap never bound. A removal of an optional patient cannot violate any hard constraint. The hard constraints fall into three cases. Some bound resource use, and a removal only frees resources. Some apply only to the removed patient's own placement. The rest concern mandatory patients (\Hc5). Feasibility is therefore
	preserved throughout. Any changed solution would be re-solved by the exact OT and NRA models and re-validated by the official validator. The baseline's theatres and nurses were themselves solved exactly. A changed plan is therefore compared with the baseline like-for-like, not under the sweep's cheaper greedy roster.

	Table~\ref{tab:audit} reports the outcome on i01 and the five instances with our largest relative
	gaps to best-known. These were chosen because a volume-driven coupling loss should be largest where
	the gap is largest, making zero removals a worst-case outcome. We picked the instances where the
	effect should show most, rather than a convenient sample, so the test carries evidence about
	the mechanism \citep{hooker1995}. No removal is ever accepted. The margin column shows how far each decision is from flipping. For every admitted optional we score $\mathrm{cost}(\text{plan without }p)-\mathrm{cost}(\text{plan with }p)$. Both states are scored under the same greedy nurse fill that the sweep uses. The exact nurse re-solve, far costlier per candidate, is reserved for accepted removals, of which there were none. A
	positive margin means keeping the patient is cheaper; a
	negative margin would mark a patient the pipeline should not have admitted. All 1\,061 margins are
	positive, which also makes the zero-removal outcome independent of the sweep order. On these instances the nurse-blind admission rule therefore makes the right call every time, as far as the audit can measure it with the greedy nurse fill. The loss from the admission--nurse link must then lie somewhere other than in how many patients we admit. Two candidates remain. The first is where patients are placed, meaning rooms and days, which is what drives continuity. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} gives that reading. The second is coordinated multi-patient withdrawals, which this one-at-a-time scope cannot test. Appendix~\ref{app:pricing} examines them.

	\section{A nurse-aware upper layer: a pre-registered test with a placebo control}\label{app:pricing}
	The audit of Appendix~\ref{app:audit} shows that no single admission is worth undoing on the instances it audits. This appendix tests the other possibility, which is where each admitted patient is placed. We add a price for the two nurse costs that can be estimated without solving the nurse model. The design is tested under a protocol written and frozen before the runs, so the results could not shape the criteria; this is the practice of pre-registration \citep{nosek2018}. Its registration time is recorded in \texttt{extensions/nurse\_pricing/PROTOCOL.md}. All code, per-run outcomes (\texttt{runs.csv}), and the post-hoc analysis script sit alongside it. The runs form a separate result set: the headline results of this paper are
	unchanged.

	\paragraph{Design.} Two terms are added to the surrogate objective, which guides the construction, the ALNS, and the moves the descent tries. The exact models are untouched and keep their own objectives (Section~\ref{sec:approach});
	every accept-or-reject test still uses the true objective, and nurses are still assigned by the unchanged, exact NRA-MIP. First, a workload term: per shift, $W_4\cdot\max(0,\ \text{workload demand} - \text{on-duty capacity})$. This term is a valid lower bound on \Sc4, since caps fill first and indivisibility only raises the true cost. It is zero whenever the nurses on duty can absorb the demanded workload. Second, a skill-scarcity term: per day,
	$W_2\cdot\max(0,\ \text{rooms whose daily requirement reaches the top skill level} -
	\text{top-skill nurses on duty})$. The day's supply is the smallest of its three shifts. We assume one top-skill room per top-skill nurse is manageable. Patient requirements enter
	per day of stay, since requirements fall as patients recover
	(the max over a whole stay is degenerate on these instances: on i01 every patient's maximum is the same level). Continuity (\Sc3) is deliberately
	not priced: at design time, Appendix~\ref{app:audit} and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} read it as a property of the roster, and no fixed price works without passing information between the layers. The budget probe
	of Appendix~\ref{app:structure} later showed that a larger nurse solve also recovers part of the
	continuity cost. A control run with both terms off reproduces the recorded baseline exactly, verified bit for bit on i01, seed 1. The added code itself therefore changes nothing, and every same-seed difference comes from the priced terms alone.

	\paragraph{Protocol.} Five instances influenced the design (i01, i02, i17, i21, i29) and are declared a development set, reported without claims. An earlier term that priced the spread of skill levels within each room-day failed on them, and the per-day skill-level check above used them. Instance i27, audited in Appendix~\ref{app:audit}, contributed no
	design decision to the pricing terms and therefore stays held out. The remaining \emph{25 instances
	are held out}: no design decision ever touched them, and all confirmatory claims rest on them
	alone. This is the standard split between data that shapes a method and data that judges it
	\citep{hastie2009}. The weights of both terms were frozen at $1.0$ with no tuning, so no result could feed back into the design \citep{nosek2018}. All 30
	instances were run on all five seeds under the production budgets, and all comparisons are
	best-of-five against best-of-five over the same seeds, with gaps total-based over the frozen
	best-known snapshot. The seeds are held fixed across both arms, so every run is paired with its own
	baseline and seed variation cannot be read as an effect of the change
	\citep{barr1995}. Every objective reported in this appendix is the official validator's, with
	zero violations across all 300 runs of both arms. We declared two success criteria in advance. Criterion (a) is the aggregate effect. The held-out best-of-5 aggregate gap must improve. Criterion (b) tests whether the effect is broad and not concentrated in a few large wins. A majority of the 125 held-out same-seed pairs must not worsen.

	\paragraph{Outcome.} Both criteria were met: the held-out aggregate gap moved from 6.78\% to
	6.39\% ($-0.39$ points), and 56 improved and 7 unchanged against 62 worse. This is the narrowest
	possible majority, and among strictly decided pairs more got worse. Post-hoc analysis (labeled
	as such: it was not pre-specified) shows the aggregate improvement is real but fragile, with a
	95\% bootstrap interval over instances of $[-0.81, -0.03]$ points (20{,}000 percentile
	resamples). The per-instance effect, though, is indistinguishable from zero (13 better, 12
	worse; two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank on relative per-instance changes, $n{=}25$, $p=0.68$).\anote{A bootstrap interval estimates how much a figure would vary if the instance set itself were drawn again \citep{efron1994}. \texttt{analyze\_posthoc.py} redraws the 25 held-out instances with replacement and recomputes the aggregate-gap change. It repeats this 20{,}000 times and reports the middle 95\% of the results. The Wilcoxon test \citep{wilcoxon1945} ranks the paired changes by absolute size and asks whether the signs lean one way. It makes no assumption about the shape of the distribution, so a few large wins cannot decide the result on their own. That suits these 25 changes. Its $p$ is the probability of a lean at least this strong if there were no true per-instance effect.} The
	gain is carried by a few large wins over the 25 held-out instances (sum $-3\,035$) against
	many small losses (sum $+548$). The pre-declared concentration prediction (larger gains where
	nurse cost dominates) is not supported by rank correlation \citep{spearman1904} (Spearman of relative change
	against nurse-cost share, $n{=}25$, $+0.20$, the wrong direction).

	\paragraph{Placebo control.} The decisive question is causal: does the improvement come from
	nurse-supply information, or would any comparable change to the numbers guiding the search do
	the same? A control that removes only the claimed ingredient answers this \citep{shadish2002}.
	The placebo has a second pre-registered protocol (\texttt{PROTOCOL\_PLACEBO.md}). We re-ran the full campaign with the identical terms priced against a shuffled roster. Each shift keeps a capacity and each day keeps a top-skill count, but drawn from the wrong shift or day. The sizes therefore stay identical, and only the timing is false. The placebo
	reproduces the gain within noise ($-0.40$ points against the nurse-informed arm's $-0.39$,
	difference $+0.006$ points with a bootstrap interval of $[-0.19, +0.19]$). By the declared decision rule, \emph{the cause is not established}: aligning the price with the true roster adds no advantage this experiment can measure, since the interval spans half the total effect. The protocol declares one limit of this control. It removes the alignment with the real roster, not the information itself. Both terms still read the true patient demand; only the supply numbers are shuffled. The follow-up control below would remove the demand information too.

	\paragraph{What both arms actually did.} A decomposition of the improvements by cost component explains
	the tie. The real patch does reduce its priced skill term about twice as much as the placebo
	($-1\,924$ versus $-929$), a trace of the intended mechanism. But the placebo reduces workload
	twice as much, and both arms share one dominant pattern, consistent with the added term working like a \emph{tax on admissions}. Over the 25 held-out instances, both arms leave more
	optional patients out (about two dozen, almost all on i19) at total unscheduled penalty
	$+4\,900$ and $+5\,400$. They gain back more than that through lower delay and nurse cost
	($-7\,470$ and $-7\,765$; the remaining components account for the small residual). This is
	consistent with Appendix~\ref{app:audit}: removing any \emph{single} admitted optional from a
	fixed plan never pays, yet a tax-induced withdrawal of several patients, re-planned around their
	absence, does. This is the coordinated withdrawal that the audit's patient-by-patient scope could not
	test (i19 itself lay outside the audited set, so the audit constrains neither reading there).
	The large $W_8$ pushes the search to admit as many patients as it can, and it seems to go a little too far. Only coordinated changes can undo that.

	\paragraph{Conclusion of the test.} A fixed price on where patients are placed, built from the supply side we tested, improves the search no more than an equal-sized change carrying no real nurse information.
	We have now measured both routes, and neither fixed nurse-informed price has beaten its own placebo. This strengthens the conclusion of Section~\ref{sec:discussion}: closing the coupling's share of the nurse gap requires the admission and nurse layers to exchange information, not a fixed surcharge. Appendix~\ref{app:structure} separates that share from the
	solver-budget share. The clean follow-up control carries no nurse information at all. The protocol names a random room-day price of matched size. For the tax reading, the most direct version is a flat per-admission surcharge. If either reproduces the $-0.4$-point shift, the effect is entirely
	the tax.

	\section{Where the nurse gap sits: a structure comparison and a budget probe}\label{app:structure}
	The component analysis of Section~\ref{sec:discussion} places most of the remaining gap in the
	three nurse terms. This appendix measures where that gap sits, in two steps, using only stored
	solutions (\texttt{extensions/structure\_diff/}, with the probe protocol, scripts, and
	per-instance records alongside). The structure metrics come from our golden-tested evaluator,
	and the probe scores come from the official validator. The runs form a separate result set; the headline numbers are unchanged.

	\paragraph{Structure comparison.} We compared the structure of our public-set solutions with
	the competition's published best solutions on the same instances \citep{ihtc2024site}. Each metric below tests one possible reason for the nurse gap, so the comparison can tell the reasons apart instead of only showing that a gap exists \citep{hooker1995}. The published best solutions do not win by lining up patients' stays or by using fewer rooms. Mean roommate stay overlap is 0.13 on both sides,\anote{The overlap of a roommate pair is the number of days both patients are in the room, divided by the number of days at least one of them is there (\texttt{compare\_structure.py}). Identical stays give 1.0 and disjoint stays give 0.} and patients per used room match (9.8 ours, 9.7 theirs). Their advantage sits in all three nurse terms, and their mean count of distinct nurses per patient is 4.95 against our 5.56. The layouts are similar, so this pointed at the nurse solve rather than the layout.

	\paragraph{Budget probe.} That reading was registered in advance in \texttt{PROBE.md}, again so the outcome could not shape the test \citep{nosek2018}; the file also records two earlier exploratory runs. We then re-solved the NRA-MIP once on each stored layout at work budget 3000, several times the production ceiling of 600 to 800, so a budget shortage could not be what limits the result. The layout stays untouched. Only the budget changes, so any improvement is attributable to it alone. This is again the one-change logic of an ablation \citep{fawcett2016}. A probe built this way answers why the gap is there. Competitive comparison against other methods cannot answer that \citep{hooker1995}. Table~\ref{tab:probe} shows the outcome. On the six largest-gap
	nurse-heavy instances the re-solve closes between 6\% and 63\% of the gap to best-known, mostly in
	continuity. The pooled share over the seven probed instances is 21.9\%, and the small control
	instance closes nothing, as the protocol predicted.

	share. The budget share is solver time that the production budgets left unused on the largest instances. The residual share is what remains for the admission--nurse coupling. Appendix~\ref{app:audit} checks one admission at a time and finds none worth undoing. Appendix~\ref{app:pricing} finds a fixed price no better than a placebo. 	\section{Held-out evaluation on the hidden instances}\label{app:hidden}
	Our development and tuning used the 30 public instances only, and every result so far was
	measured on them. The competition kept a second set of 30 hidden instances for its final
	evaluation and has since published both the instances and the best solutions its finalists
	reached. A single campaign of the frozen pipeline on that set measures generalization directly,
	the standard role of a held-out set \citep{hastie2009}.
	The protocol (\texttt{extensions/hidden\_eval/PROTOCOL\_HIDDEN.md}) was registered before any run, so no result could shape it \citep{nosek2018}. It fixed the code, the budgets, and the five-seed best-of-five policy. It also declared that we would report the outcome, whatever it was, and give the feasibility and runtime of every run \citep{barr1995}. The benchmarks are the published best
	solutions, re-scored by the official validator; the values match the UB column of the
	competition paper \citep{ihtc2024paper} on every instance we spot-checked.

	\paragraph{Outcome.} All 150 runs are feasible under the official validator. The total-based
	aggregate gap to the published best solutions is 6.10\%, against 7.80\% on the public set.
	Thirteen of the 30 instances fall within 3\% and 18 within 5\%, and the largest gaps are again
	on nurse-heavy instances (17.8\% on m27). The set includes instances larger than any public one
	(m29 has 631 patients). Table~\ref{tab:hidden} gives the per-instance outcomes.

	\paragraph{Two caveats.} The hidden benchmarks come from the five finalist methods, each run
	ten times by the organizers \citep{ihtc2024paper}, while the public best-known values pool the
	submissions of 32 teams. The hidden benchmarks may therefore be easier to approach, and the two aggregate gaps
	are not strictly comparable. One campaign also cannot show how much the result would vary if we ran it again; the per-seed records are in \texttt{extensions/hidden\_eval/}. With those limits stated, the method's performance carries to instances it never saw, at a similar gap level, with feasibility on every run.
